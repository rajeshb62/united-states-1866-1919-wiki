Overview

Wall’s Andrew Carnegie is a full-scale biography of Carnegie from Dunfermline childhood through immigration, Pittsburgh telegraph and railroad work, Civil War opportunities, diversified investment, bridge and iron ventures, the rise of Carnegie Steel, the Homestead crisis, sale to United States Steel, and the long philanthropic and peace-seeking afterlife of his fortune. The book is organized in three broad movements: The Charter (origins and migration), The Contract (business career, 1853–1901), and The Deed (philanthropy, empire of giving, peace, and final years, 1899–1919).

For this United States 1866–1919 wiki, the source is central for industrial capitalism, steel, Pittsburgh, railroad-linked entrepreneurship, labor conflict, philanthropy, anti-imperial politics, elite transatlantic networks, libraries, higher education, peace activism, and the moral self-fashioning of a Gilded Age capitalist. It pairs naturally with Titan for oil/steel comparisons, with A Life of George Westinghouse for Pittsburgh industrial technology, and with The Wizard of Menlo Park for fame, invention, and public reputation.

The raw text is usable but noisy in the scanned contents pages: chapter headings and body text are mostly readable, while some table-of-contents lines are garbled by OCR. Chapter body starts are clear enough for mining.

Coverage Guide

Carnegie, steel, and post-Civil War capitalism

Chapters VI–XX are the core business arc. They show Carnegie’s rise through Pennsylvania Railroad patronage, Civil War logistical opportunities, postwar investment, iron bridges, bonds, Bessemer steel, Edgar Thomson, Carnegie Steel, Frick, Homestead, vertical integration, and sale to United States Steel. This is the steel counterpart to Rockefeller’s oil empire in Titan.

Labor conflict and Homestead

Chapters XV and XVI are the key sections for a future Homestead event page. They connect Frick, coke, wage conflict, lockout, Pinkertons, violence, public opinion, and Carnegie’s own absentee responsibility. Chapter XVII then shows how Carnegie Steel expanded after the crisis.

Philanthropy and the Gospel of Wealth

Chapters XXI–XXIII are the strongest material for Carnegie’s public doctrine and institutional giving: free libraries, church organs, Scottish universities, Dunfermline, Carnegie Institute, Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, and peace projects. This material complements Rockefeller philanthropy in Titan, but Carnegie’s giving is more explicitly tied to his published moral theory of surplus wealth.

Pittsburgh and industrial technology

Carnegie’s Pittsburgh, railroad, bridge, iron, steel, and finance career pairs with A Life of George Westinghouse, another Pittsburgh-centered industrial biography. Together they can support pages on Pittsburgh industry, rail-connected entrepreneurship, steel/electrical infrastructure, and the city’s role in Gilded Age industrial systems.

Public persona, writing, and transatlantic networks

Chapters XIV, XVIII, XXIII, and XXIV are useful for Carnegie’s two-world life: industrialist and man of letters, Scot and American, businessman and public moralist, anti-imperialist and peace advocate, Skibo host and global celebrity. This public persona can be compared with Edison’s technological celebrity in The Wizard of Menlo Park.

Notes on Scope

This is a modern scholarly biography, not a period memoir. It is nevertheless dense with letters, business history, public controversy, institutional philanthropy, and political context. Its post-1919 material should be treated as legacy and continuation of Carnegie’s institutions rather than as core chronological evidence for the 1866–1919 wiki.
