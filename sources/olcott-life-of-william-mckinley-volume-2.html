Completeness Assessment

The source appears complete as Volume II of a two-volume biography, not as the entire biography. The OCR ZIP sorts naturally from 00000001.txt through 00000472.txt and includes the Volume II title/copyright pages, contents, illustrations list, Chapters XXIV–XXXV, appendix, and index. Blank scan pages and a few OCR artifacts are present, but the contents and back matter run through the end of the volume.

Because this is Volume II, it begins with McKinley’s presidency already underway. It does not contain the earlier chapters on McKinley’s childhood, Civil War service, Ohio politics, congressional career, governorship, tariff politics, or the 1896 election except through index references back to Volume I.

Overview

Olcott’s second volume is a presidency-centered biography of William McKinley from the Cuban crisis through his assassination and memorialization. Its strongest material for this wiki is the transition from continental post-Civil War politics into overseas war and imperial administration: intervention in Cuba, the Spanish-American War, the Treaty of Paris, the Philippines, Cuba and Puerto Rico, China and the Open Door, and McKinley’s reelection in 1900.

The source should be used as a period biography rather than a neutral diplomatic monograph. It is sympathetic to McKinley, often defending his caution, judgment, and administrative balance. That makes it valuable for contemporary and near-contemporary pro-McKinley framing, especially when paired with The House of Morgan for finance and empire-era diplomacy, Andrew Carnegie for anti-imperial and public moral context, Titan for late-Gilded-Age Republican political economy, and Ford: The Times, the Man, the Company for the industrial and international-commercial world into which McKinley’s final Buffalo speech looked forward.

Narrative Source Flow

Front Matter and Illustrations

Lines 19–171. The front matter identifies this as Volume II of Olcott’s biography and provides a contents list from Chapter XXIV through Chapter XXXV. The illustrations center the public presidency: McKinley at Buffalo, the peace protocol, a memorandum on Dewey and the Philippines, the cabinet, military figures, the 1900 acceptance speech, Ida Saxton McKinley, Canton, and memorial sites.

Chapter XXIV — Intervention

Lines 187–1166. The volume opens with the pressures that pushed the United States toward intervention in Cuba: sympathy for Cuban suffering, sanitary fears, trade and investment losses, hazards to American residents, neutrality enforcement, and doubts that Spain could end the war. Olcott emphasizes McKinley’s reluctance to rush into war, his interest in giving Spain’s Sagasta ministry time, and his movement toward forcible intervention only after diplomacy and autonomy schemes failed.

Chapter XXV — The Spanish-American War

Lines 1167–1994. This chapter covers congressional resolutions, war authority, the Teller Amendment, the opening of hostilities, strategy, naval and military preparations, Dewey’s orders and victory at Manila, operations around Cuba, and the American war effort. It presents McKinley as coordinating reluctant but increasingly decisive war policy from Washington.

Chapter XXVI — The End of the War

Lines 1995–2654. Olcott follows the closing stage of the war after Santiago: Spanish capitulation, peace feelers, the protocol, occupation questions, and the rapid collapse of Spain’s military position. The chapter is useful for tracing how battlefield success translated into diplomatic bargaining and administrative burdens.

Chapter XXVII — A Wave of Criticism

Lines 2655–3212. The war’s military success is followed by criticism of army administration, camp conditions, disease, supply failures, and public controversy. Olcott treats the controversy as a test of wartime organization and presidential responsibility, making this chapter a useful source for the limits of late-nineteenth-century federal military administration.

Chapter XXVIII — The Peace Negotiations

Lines 3213–4875. McKinley appoints William R. Day, Cushman K. Davis, William P. Frye, George Gray, and Whitelaw Reid as peace commissioners. The chapter tracks the Paris negotiations, the central question of the Philippines, relations with European powers, the changing attitude of Britain, and the Senate fight over ratification.

Chapter XXIX — The Making of the Philippines

Lines 4876–6615. Olcott moves from the Spanish colonial crisis into American Philippine policy. The chapter covers revolt, Aguinaldo, Dewey, Manila, instructions and commissions, the problem of sovereignty, native government, insurrection, and McKinley’s reasoning about American responsibility in the islands.

Chapter XXX — The New Government in the Antilles

Lines 6616–7506. This chapter covers Cuba and Puerto Rico after the Treaty of Paris. It follows military occupation, obligations assumed by the United States, the Foraker Act and Puerto Rican tariff issues, constitutional questions, and the practical problem of converting wartime occupation into civil administration.

Chapter XXXI — China

Lines 7507–8899. The chapter treats the China crisis and Open Door policy during McKinley’s presidency. It covers John Hay’s illness and policy role, Boxer violence, protection of the legations, instructions to General Chaffee, the American effort to preserve Chinese territorial integrity, and the final settlement that kept the Open Door from becoming formal partition.

Chapter XXXII — Renomination and Reelection

Lines 8900–10467. Olcott places the 1900 election in the changed national mood after prosperity, war victory, and imperial debate. The chapter follows the Republican renomination, vice-presidential politics, Theodore Roosevelt’s nomination, Bryan and Democratic opposition, free silver and imperialism, front-porch campaigning, and McKinley’s reelection.

Chapter XXXIII — Looking Forward

Lines 10468–11062. The second inaugural and early second term are treated as a moment of enlarged national confidence. Olcott emphasizes McKinley’s changed stature after four years in office, his search for conciliation, and his forward-looking program of prosperity, reciprocity, international trade, peace, and national responsibility.

Chapter XXXIV — The Tragedy at Buffalo

Lines 11063–11738. This chapter narrates McKinley’s visit to the Pan-American Exposition, the public reception at Buffalo, Leon Czolgosz’s attack, the medical crisis, public anxiety, the president’s death, and the emotional national response. It is the principal source section for the assassination and transition to mourning.

Chapter XXXV — Conclusion

Lines 11739–13142. Olcott closes with an explicit appraisal of McKinley’s character, leadership, public virtues, and place in national memory. The conclusion is strongly commemorative and should be used as evidence of the post-assassination McKinley memorial tradition as much as of the presidency itself.

Appendix and Index

Lines 13143–16821. The appendix preserves McKinley’s last public address at Buffalo on September 5, 1901, including his statements on expositions, international commerce, modern communications, prosperity, foreign markets, reciprocity, and the impossibility of isolation. The index runs through the end of the OCR and is especially useful because it points back to Volume I entries as well as Volume II subjects.

Coverage Guide

Spanish-American War and Intervention

Chapters XXIV–XXVI form the intervention and war core: Cuba, Spain, Congress, naval preparedness, Dewey, Santiago, protocol, and the shift from humanitarian and commercial concern to formal war. These sections should feed future pages on the Spanish-American War, Cuban intervention, Teller Amendment, and McKinley’s war leadership.

Empire, Islands, and Constitutional Questions

Chapters XXVIII–XXX cover the postwar settlement: Treaty of Paris, Philippines, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Foraker Act, occupation, civil government, and constitutional uncertainty about new possessions. These chapters support future pages on American empire, Philippine-American War context, Puerto Rico, Cuba under U.S. occupation, and the Insular Cases background.

China and Open Door Diplomacy

Chapter XXXI is the China-policy core. It gives a presidency-centered account of the Boxer crisis, legation danger, John Hay’s Open Door diplomacy, Chaffee’s expedition, and the American effort to preserve Chinese integrity without partition.

Election of 1900 and Republican Politics

Chapter XXXII links prosperity, imperialism, Bryan, Roosevelt, Republican confidence, and McKinley’s reelection. It should feed future pages on the election of 1900, Republican Party politics, Bryan’s anti-imperial campaign, and Roosevelt’s move toward national power.

McKinley’s Final Public Program

Chapter XXXIII and the appendix are especially valuable for McKinley’s late program: reciprocity, trade outlets, modern transportation and communications, arbitration, prosperity, and the claim that isolation was no longer possible or desirable.

Assassination and Memorialization

Chapters XXXIV–XXXV and the illustrations capture the assassination at Buffalo, Ida Saxton McKinley, public mourning, Canton memorial culture, and the commemorative presentation of McKinley as soldier, executive, unifier, and moral exemplar.

Notes on Scope

This source is a sympathetic 1916 biography, close enough to McKinley’s generation to preserve commemorative language and administration-friendly interpretation. It should be mined for McKinley-era narrative, speeches, and policy framing, while later synthetic pages should balance it against other sources where possible.
