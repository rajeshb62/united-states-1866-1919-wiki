Overview

T. J. Stiles’s The First Tycoon is the definitive modern biography of Cornelius Vanderbilt (1794–1877), the self-made Staten Island ferryman who became the richest man in nineteenth-century America — a fortune so vast that at his death he held roughly one out of every twenty dollars in national circulation, including cash and demand deposits. The book opens not with Vanderbilt’s birth but with the 1877 will contest in Surrogate Court, where hundreds of spectators and reporters crammed into a Manhattan courtroom to hear the secrets of the Commodore’s life; this trial, which dragged on for two years, two months, and four days, generated the fragmented mosaic of fact and legend that Stiles sets out to correct.

Stiles traces Vanderbilt’s career through three distinct phases. Part One (1794–1847) follows his rise from a rough-handed boy on Staten Island, through his breakthrough partnership with Thomas Gibbons in the steamboat war against the Livingston-Fulton monopoly, to the landmark Supreme Court case Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) that tore down state-erected barriers to interstate commerce and helped remake the Constitution. Vanderbilt emerges as a “captain” in his own right — aggressive, competitive, personally commanding, and ruthlessly efficient.

Part Two (1848–1860) covers the California gold rush years, when Vanderbilt created the Nicaragua transit route — the Accessory Transit Company — that carried fortune-seekers across the Central American isthmus faster and cheaper than the Panama alternative. These years saw his epic rivalry with Cornelius K. Garrison, Charles Morgan, and the filibuster William Walker, whom Vanderbilt ultimately destroyed by cutting off his supply lines. By the end of this period Vanderbilt had shifted from steamboats to ocean steamers, building the largest private fleet in the world, and had begun to turn his attention to railroads.

Part Three (1861–1877) follows Vanderbilt’s transformation into a railroad king. During the Civil War he put his fleet and fortune at the Union’s disposal, then consolidated the fragmented railways serving New York City into the New York Central and Hudson River Railroad system. His construction of Grand Central Depot (the predecessor of today’s Grand Central Terminal) and the four-track trunk line up the Hudson Valley created an integrated transportation network that transformed the American economy. Stiles argues that Vanderbilt left his most lasting mark in the “invisible world” of finance, helping to create the modern corporate form, the securities market, and the abstract conception of capital that later generations would take for granted.

The extract begins with front matter (Also By, Dedication, Rusdie epigraph, Contents, List of Illustrations, List of Maps) and concludes with extensive endnotes (numbered by chapter), a Bibliographical Essay, and a Primary Source Bibliography. Full Chapter-by-Chapter Breakdown

Chapter One — The Islander

They came to learn his secrets. The chapter opens in Surrogate Court, Manhattan, November 12, 1877 — the trial over the will of Cornelius Vanderbilt. Hundreds of spectators, fashionably dressed, crowd the courtroom to hear the details of the richest man’s life. William H. Vanderbilt, the eldest son, faces his sister Mary Vanderbilt La Bau’s challenge to the will. The New York Times headlines the stakes: “A BATTLE OVER $100,000,000.” From here, Stiles backtracks to Vanderbilt’s origins.

Cornelius Vanderbilt was born on May 27, 1794, on Staten Island, into a “thin, hard, rudely simple” Dutch-descended farming family. His father, also Cornelius, was coarse, illiterate, and violent; his mother, Phebe Hand, was shrewd and commanding. The young Vanderbilt grew up in a semi-frontier of hard work, physical contests, and rough independence. He left school at eleven and at sixteen persuaded his mother to lend him $100 to buy a periauger (a two-masted shallow-draft sailing vessel) to work the ferry route between Staten Island and Manhattan. By age eighteen he commanded men twice his age, built a thriving freight and passenger business, and married his first cousin, Sophia Johnson, who became the steady hand behind his domestic and commercial affairs. The chapter ends with his first encounter with steam power and the decision — at twenty-three — to take command of Thomas Gibbons’s steamboat, the Mouse.

Chapter Two — The Duelist

The twenty-three-year-old Vanderbilt takes command of the Mouse on November 24, 1817, in the decisive decision of his life. Thomas Gibbons, a nearly three-hundred-pound Georgia planter, lawyer, and former Loyalist, had moved to Elizabethtown, New Jersey, and entered the steamboat trade in defiance of the Livingston-Fulton monopoly that controlled New York waters. Stiles recounts Gibbons’s character — pathologically contentious, a duelist who had exchanged gunfire with opponents three times, a man “that could not be led.” Gibbons had hired a young captain named Vanderbilt to run his steamboat in the face of the monopoly’s legal harassment.

The chapter details the legal and physical war between Gibbons and the monopoly. The New York legislature had granted Robert Fulton and Robert R. Livingston exclusive rights to steam navigation in New York waters in 1808; Gibbons, running his boat from New Jersey to Manhattan, was repeatedly sued, arrested, and fined. Vanderbilt piloted the boat in and out of the city, dodging process-servers and outrunning the monopoly’s boats, learning every channel and tide. The heart of the chapter is the legal struggle culminating in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), argued by Daniel Webster and William Wirt before Chief Justice John Marshall, who declared the federal power over interstate commerce supreme, shattering the state monopoly system. Vanderbilt emerges as Gibbons’s indispensable man — fearless, skilled, and fiercely loyal.

Chapter Three — A Tricky God

The aftermath of Gibbons v. Ogden. With the monopoly dead, Vanderbilt expected to become master of his own steamboat, but Gibbons refused to release him from his contract. Vanderbilt chafed under Gibbons’s command even as Gibbons’s health declined. The chapter follows Gibbons’s final years — his second family with a young maid, his legal and financial battles, his death in 1826. Vanderbilt, anticipating his freedom, had already begun planning his own independent operations. He built his first steamboat, the Bellona, on spec, designing it for speed and economy. But Gibbons’s executor, and Gibbons’s son William, kept Vanderbilt entangled for years.

After Gibbons’s death, Vanderbilt formed a partnership with Gibbons’s son that quickly soured. The younger Gibbons, untalented and arrogant, tried to dictate terms. Vanderbilt, now thirty-two, broke away and began running his own boats. The chapter closes with Vanderbilt establishing himself as an independent line on the Hudson, the Raritan, and Long Island Sound — still a small operator among many, but fiercely ambitious.

Chapter Four — Nemesis

Vanderbilt’s years of struggle in the 1830s. He built and ran steamboats on the Hudson, competing with the heavily capitalized lines of the Hudson River Steamboat Association. His methods were brutal: he cut fares to ruinous lows, ran faster schedules, and personally piloted his boats in storms to build a reputation for reliability. The chapter introduces his great rival, Daniel Drew — a former cattle drover turned steamboat captain who matched Vanderbilt in craftiness. Drew and Vanderbilt fought a rate war on the Hudson that bled both men.

The panic of 1837 hit Vanderbilt hard. He had overextended, buying boats on credit, and the financial collapse left him struggling. His wife Sophia sold household goods to keep the family going. Vanderbilt sold his boats to the Association at the bottom of the market, took a job running their boats for a salary, and quietly rebuilt. The chapter also explores Vanderbilt’s domestic life: his growing family, his rigid discipline at home, his perpetual absences. Sophia emerges as a resilient figure who managed a household of twelve children largely alone.

Chapter Five — Sole Control

Vanderbilt’s return to independent operation and his conquest of the Long Island Sound route. By the early 1840s he had clawed his way back, buying his own boats, and turned his attention to the lucrative overnight run between New York and Boston via Long Island Sound — a route dominated by the heavily capitalized Norwich and Worcester Railroad. Vanderbilt built a line around the competition, cutting rates, building faster boats, and pioneering the concept of the “safety” line (advertising accident-free records). He drove the opposition into bankruptcy or combination.

The chapter captures Vanderbilt at his most commercially Darwinian. “I have always served the public to the best of my ability,” he said, “but I have never been such a fool as to run a boat without making money.” By 1846 he was the dominant force on the Sound. His reputation for paranoia and secrecy grew: he kept no ledgers, burned correspondence, and trusted only his son William (whom he kept under harsh discipline). The chapter ends with Vanderbilt, still only fifty-three, already calling himself “Commodore,” but restless.

Chapter Six — Man of Honor

Vanderbilt’s move into oceangoing steamships and the California gold rush. The discovery of gold at Sutter’s Mill in January 1848 set off a frenzy. Tens of thousands poured into California, most going by sea around Cape Horn — a six-month voyage — or via the Panama isthmus. Vanderbilt saw an opportunity: instead of Panama, he would open a route across Nicaragua, using the San Juan River and Lake Nicaragua, with a short land portage. It would cut the transit time by weeks.

The chapter describes Vanderbilt’s furious campaign to build his Nicaragua line. He commissioned four ocean steamers, organized a company in Nicaragua, and sent surveyors to map the route. The key was a charter from the Nicaraguan government, which he obtained through a mix of bribes, political maneuvering, and sheer force of will. The Accessory Transit Company was born. Vanderbilt personally supervised the construction of the riverboats and the road across the isthmus. An immense figure — six feet tall, powerfully built, commanding in a frock coat and beaver hat — he embodied the rush-era energy. By 1851 his line was carrying forty thousand passengers a year.

Chapter Seven — Prometheus

The Accessory Transit Company in operation. Vanderbilt’s Nicaragua line transformed travel to California: a journey that took six months around Cape Horn could now be made in under a month. The chapter details the engineering and logistical challenges — steamers powered by walking-beam engines, shallow-draft riverboats that could navigate the San Juan rapids, heat and tropical disease that killed crewmen. Vanderbilt’s rival, the Panama Railroad Company (backed by Wall Street), slashed its own rates in response, triggering a furious rate war.

Stiles uses this chapter to draw a portrait of Vanderbilt’s business methods: his instinct for timing, his willingness to spend huge sums to crush competitors, his contempt for formal organization. The Transit Company was a corporation on paper but a personal fiefdom in practice — Vanderbilt dominated its board, booked its profits through his own accounts, and kept its structure deliberately opaque. The chapter also introduces Cornelius K. Garrison and Charles Morgan, who would become his antagonists in the next phase. The gold-rush boom fed Vanderbilt’s fortune: by 1853 he was worth an estimated $11 million, the largest individual fortune in the United States.

Chapter Eight — Star of the West

The Nicaraguan transit line’s vulnerability and Vanderbilt’s confrontation with the filibuster William Walker. By the mid-1850s, Walker — a Tennessee-born adventurer and professional revolutionary — had seized control of Nicaragua, declaring himself president. He promptly revoked the Transit Company’s charter and granted it to Vanderbilt’s rivals, Garrison and Morgan, who had conspired with Walker in a hostile takeover. Vanderbilt was ousted from his own company.

Rather than negotiate, Vanderbilt launched a methodical campaign of destruction. He used his remaining political and financial connections to lobby Washington against recognizing Walker’s regime. He bribed Walker’s officers. He personally underwrote the military campaign of the Central American coalition that eventually drove Walker out — a war funded largely by Vanderbilt’s own cash. The chapter culminates in Walker’s surrender to a U.S. Navy captain in 1857 and Vanderbilt’s triumphant return to the Transit Company. But the filibuster episode had changed him: he now saw political power as entwined with commerce, and he began to look beyond steamboats.

Chapter Nine — North Star

Vanderbilt’s grand gesture: the building of the North Star, a steam yacht of unprecedented luxury, and his European tour of 1853. He took his family — Sophia, several children, and a retinue of servants — on a grand tour of Britain and the Continent. The yacht was a statement: the self-made man of humble Staten Island origins now sailed the Mediterranean as an American prince.

Stiles uses the tour to probe Vanderbilt’s character. He visited the Great Exhibition in London, toured the factories of Birmingham and Manchester, and studied British railways — a technology that would soon absorb him. But the tour also revealed his limits: he was uncomfortable in European society, bored by art and architecture, and contemptuous of aristocracy. He cut the tour short and returned home, more confirmed than ever in his American commercial values. The chapter also covers his growing interest in railroads, which he saw as the logical extension of steamboat transport.

Chapter Ten — Ariel

Vanderbilt’s final years in the steamship business and his transition to railroads. The chapter recounts the epic rivalry on the Atlantic steamer route, where Vanderbilt launched the Vanderbilt — the largest steamship afloat — against the Collins Line and the British Cunard Line. He was driven by both commercial instinct and wounded pride: the British government subsidized its lines, and Vanderbilt wanted to prove that American private enterprise could beat them.

The Atlantic steamship venture was a financial disaster — Vanderbilt lost over a million dollars — but it was also a strategic move. By demonstrating that he could and would sacrifice huge sums, he established himself as a man not to be crossed. The chapter closes with Vanderbilt selling off his steamship interests and buying into the New York & Harlem Railroad. He was sixty-one years old, and the railroads of New York were about to meet their master.

Chapter Eleven — Vanderbilt

Vanderbilt enters the railroad world in earnest. The chapter is titled for the man himself — a turning point where his reputation became his weapon. In 1862 he began quietly buying shares of the New York & Harlem Railroad, then a battered line that ran from Manhattan north to Chatham Four Corners. He saw what others did not: the Harlem’s right-of-way into the city gave it strategic value out of proportion to its current earnings.

The Harlem’s board put up a fight. The chapter details Vanderbilt’s patient accumulation of stock, his cultivation of board allies, and his first test of power. By 1863 he controlled the Harlem. He immediately began upgrading the line, replacing iron rails with steel, double-tracking, cutting grades, and extending the line. The chapter also covers Daniel Drew’s parallel rise in the Erie Railroad — the old rivals were now on parallel tracks, heading for collision. The outbreak of the Civil War frames the background: Vanderbilt’s fleet was chartered by the Union, and his son William managed the war contracts.

Chapter Twelve — Champion

The Harlem corner of 1863 — Vanderbilt’s first great railroad battle. A coalition of speculators, led by Drew and the Erie crowd, decided to corner Harlem stock by selling short, expecting the stock to fall. Vanderbilt had been quietly buying up every share. When the shorts tried to cover, they discovered that Vanderbilt owned the floating supply. The stock rocketed from 90 to 179 in a few days; the shorts were wiped out. Vanderbilt made a fortune and established his dominance.

But the victory was ambiguous. The chapter explores Vanderbilt’s use of the stock market as a weapon — cornering stock, bribing legislators, and manipulating news — and the moral ambiguity of his methods. He saw himself as a defender of the honest investor against speculators, but his own tactics were indistinguishable from theirs. The chapter also covers the Harlem’s continued expansion and the beginning of his systematic consolidation of the New York Central Railroad, his masterwork.

Chapter Thirteen — War

The Civil War years and Vanderbilt’s growing role in the Union war effort. He chartered his largest oceangoing steamer, also named the Vanderbilt, to the Navy Department for conversion into a cruiser. He eventually donated it outright — a gift worth over $800,000 — and Secretary of the Navy Gideon Welles accepted it for the blockade of the Confederacy. Vanderbilt also organized a syndicate to transport California gold to the East, keeping specie flowing to the Treasury.

The chapter details Vanderbilt’s relationship with the Lincoln administration. He was a Democrat and personally contemptuous of politicians, but he supported the Union. His son William ran the day-to-day management of the railroads, freeing Vanderbilt to maneuver strategically. The chapter also covers the growing cost of the war and Vanderbilt’s quiet accumulation of stock in the Hudson River Railroad, the next prize in his consolidation plan. By the end of the war, Vanderbilt controlled the Harlem, the Hudson River, and the New York Central — the three lines that would form his empire.

Chapter Fourteen — The Origins of Empire

Vanderbilt’s consolidation of the New York Central and Hudson River railroads into a unified system. The chapter details the structural fragmentation of American railroads in the 1860s — dozens of short lines, each with its own station, schedule, and gauge — and Vanderbilt’s systematic strategy to unite them. He bought the Hudson River Railroad in 1864 and merged it with the Harlem and New York Central lines, creating a continuous rail route from New York City to Buffalo.

The chapter explores Vanderbilt’s physical transformation of the system: the construction of the four-track Grand Central Depot at 42nd Street (the first central passenger terminal in New York), the double-tracking of the Hudson River line through the Palisades, and the standardization of equipment and operations across his properties. He cut costs ruthlessly, eliminated duplicate stations, and slashed passenger and freight rates. The result was a transportation network of unprecedented efficiency — and Vanderbilt emerged as the closest thing to a monopoly. The chapter also covers his relationship with Tammany Hall, the New York state legislature, and the judges whose favor he purchased.

Chapter Fifteen — The Power to Punish

The Erie War of 1867–1868 — one of the most spectacular financial battles of the nineteenth century. Daniel Drew, now treasurer of the Erie Railroad, had been issuing fraudulent stock, diluting the value of shares. Vanderbilt, who had been buying Erie stock as an investment, discovered the fraud and determined to take control of the Erie. Drew, allied with Jay Gould and James Fisk, outmaneuvered Vanderbilt with the legendary “Erie printing press” — issuing thousands of new shares from a printing press in a locked room.

The chapter is a full account of the Erie War: the rush of process-servers, bribed judges, competing injunctions, and Vanderbilt’s discovery that he had bought millions of dollars in counterfeit stock. He retaliated by trying to buy a law from the New York legislature that would legalize the Erie consolidation — and was defeated by Gould’s even heftier bribes. The chapter ends with Vanderbilt’s humiliation: he had been outmaneuvered and lost millions. But the defeat hardened his resolve. He retreated from the Erie fight and focused on his own railroads, making them stronger than ever.

Chapter Sixteen — Among Friends

Vanderbilt’s family life, his relationship with his children, and the dynastic struggles that would culminate in the will contest. The chapter introduces the full cast of Vanderbilt children and the tensions among them. Cornelius Jeremiah (“Corneil”), epileptic and dissolute, was the favorite of his mother and the despair of his father. William H., the dutiful elder son, was kept under harsh discipline for decades and only earned his father’s respect through competence.

The chapter covers Sophia Vanderbilt’s death in 1868, a loss that shook the Commodore. He remarried within a year — Frank Armstrong Crawford, a cousin forty-three years his junior — a marriage that scandalized his children and alienated his daughter Mary. The chapter also details Vanderbilt’s donation to found Vanderbilt University in Nashville in 1873, a gift of $1 million that Bishop Holland McTyeire of the Methodist Episcopal Church South had solicited through Frank’s family connections. Bishop McTyeire became Vanderbilt’s most trusted confidant in his final years.

Chapter Seventeen — Consolidations

Vanderbilt’s final decade: the expansion and integration of the New York Central system. The chapter covers the acquisition of the Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway (1873), which gave Vanderbilt a continuous line to Chicago. He now controlled the most important rail corridor in America. His trunk line — from New York City through Albany, Buffalo, Cleveland, Toledo, and Chicago — moved grain, livestock, coal, and manufactured goods on a scale that made the Erie Canal look like a ditch.

The chapter also covers Vanderbilt’s battle with the New York State legislature over taxation and rate regulation. He defended his empire with characteristic ruthlessness, bribing legislators and judges when necessary, but also made concessions when the political tide turned. The panic of 1873 devastated the American economy but Vanderbilt’s railroads, built with low debt and high efficiency, survived. He emerged from the depression richer than ever. The chapter ends with Vanderbilt, now approaching eighty, beginning to transfer control to William.

Chapter Eighteen — Dynasty

The transition of power to William H. Vanderbilt and the inheritance that would trigger the will contest. The chapter details the gradual, methodical transfer of railroad presidencies and board seats. William, who had spent decades in his father’s shadow, proved to be an exceptional administrator — more systematic and patient than the Commodore, and better at managing the day-to-day operation of the system.

The chapter explores the elder Vanderbilt’s loneliness in old age: his children estranged, his friends dead, his wife Frank’s attentions failing. He spent his final years at the house on Washington Place, attended by physicians and spiritualists, reading the Bible and the New York newspapers. He died of peritonitis from a perforated colon on January 4, 1877. The chapter ends on the moment of death — William’s telegrams to Bishop McTyeire — and the immediate eruption of the will contest.

Epilogue — They Never Learned His Secrets

The conclusion of the will trial and Stiles’s summation of Vanderbilt’s historical significance. The trial lasted two years, two months, and four days. William won, doubling the shares of his siblings. But Stiles argues that the trial’s sensationalism obscured the real man. Vanderbilt’s significance, he contends, lay in his contribution to the creation of a national economy: the destruction of state trade barriers, the integration of transportation networks, the growth of New York as a financial capital, and the emergence of the modern corporation.

Stiles offers a sharp historiographical judgment: Vanderbilt has been caricatured as a robber baron, but his impact at a fundamental level over a formative period was arguably unmatched. The invisible architecture of modern economic life — abstract capital, fluctuating securities, corporate personhood — that later generations would take for granted had its origins in Vanderbilt’s world. The epilogue ends with the observation that Vanderbilt’s own desire for privacy was vindicated: “They never learned his secrets.”
