Overview

Prout’s A Life of George Westinghouse is a technically oriented biography published under the auspices of The American Society of Mechanical Engineers. The preface explains that Westinghouse left little private writing—almost no private letters, journals, notebooks, addresses, or papers—so the book was built from company files and the memories of men who had worked closely with him. Because Westinghouse dealt simultaneously with air brakes, electric power, natural gas, engines, signalling, finance, and foreign companies, Prout deliberately avoids a simple chronological structure and instead treats each field separately.

For this United States 1866–1919 wiki, the source is strongest on railroad safety, the air brake, the friction draft gear, alternating current, transformers, induction motors, Niagara power, electric traction, turbines, natural gas in Pittsburgh, European industrial expansion, and Westinghouse corporate finance. It is a period-proximate institutional biography: admiring and commemorative, but rich in technical detail and in the Westinghouse network of engineers, managers, companies, patents, and industrial systems.

Coverage Guide

Railroad safety and transportation

Chapters II, III, and XII are the strongest material for railroad technology. The air brake chapter is the largest single section and follows the move from early brake ideas through automatic operation, triple valves, trials, quick action, English experiments, and railroad adoption. The friction draft gear and signalling/interlocking chapters show Westinghouse’s broader transportation-safety system beyond braking.

Alternating current and electric power

Chapters IV–IX and XI are central for the alternating-current story. They cover transformers, Tesla’s induction motor, meters, rotary converters, Chicago World’s Fair, Niagara Falls, electric traction, and turbo-generators. These sections pair naturally with The Wizard of Menlo Park because Edison and Westinghouse appear as rival but complementary figures in the making of American electrical infrastructure.

Industrial organization and corporate expansion

The company chart, Chapters XV and XVI, and the appendix provide material on Westinghouse’s associated companies, global industrial organization, financing, receivership, reorganization, and relation to bankers. This should be read beside Titan when comparing integrated industrial empires, technical corporations, finance, and post-Civil War American capitalism.

Pittsburgh, natural gas, and urban industry

Chapter XIII is useful for Pittsburgh industrial history: natural gas, smoke reduction, fuel substitution, safety dangers, and the Philadelphia Company. It adds urban and infrastructural detail that differs from the rail and electric-power chapters.

Westinghouse as person and institutional memory

The preface, Chapter I, Chapter XVII, and Chapter XVIII are the best material for a George Westinghouse entity page: family, Civil War, shop education, home life, worker relations, ethical reputation, managerial style, humanitarian policies, and the commemorative meaning ASME attached to him.

Notes on Scope

This is an admiring institutional biography by an engineering society, and it tends to emphasize achievement, public usefulness, and Westinghouse’s constructive character. Its technical and corporate detail is nevertheless highly valuable. For interpretive balance, pair it with Edison material in The Wizard of Menlo Park and with industrial-capitalism/finance material in Titan.
