The ZIP contains 778 page .txt files sorting naturally from 00000001.txt through 00000778.txt; the joined raw file includes front matter, title/copyright pages, preface, full contents, illustrations list, all twenty-two chapters, notes, ten appendices, bibliography, and index. There are expected OCR artifacts and some blank or image-caption pages, but the book structure is intact and the final pages continue through the index rather than stopping mid-text.

This is the first volume of the Nevins/Hill Ford history, so it is complete as this volume rather than as the whole Ford story. Its chronological endpoint is the company and Ford by roughly 1916–1918, with Chapter XXII explicitly focused on rising dangers in Ford’s character, advisers, and autocratic control. That scope fits the United States 1866–1919 wiki especially well.

Overview

Nevins frames the automobile not as an isolated invention but as part of a deeper transformation in roads, transport, mechanical skill, factory organization, and American mass production. The preface says the book is simultaneously a history of Ford Motor Company, a partial biography of Henry Ford, and a broader history of the early automotive industry. It draws heavily on Ford Motor Company Archives, oral histories, Detroit Public Library collections, University of Michigan materials, legal records, trade periodicals, and contemporary newspapers.

For this wiki, the source is central for Henry Ford, Detroit, the automobile industry, mass production, Model T, Ford Motor Company, the Selden patent case, labor welfare policy, the five-dollar day, and the transformation of industrial work before World War I. It pairs naturally with The Wizard of Menlo Park for Edison and inventor-celebrity context, with A Life of George Westinghouse for machinery and industrial technology, with The House of Morgan for finance-capital and corporate consolidation, and with Andrew Carnegie for comparison between steel-era and automobile-era industrial organization.

Narrative Source Flow

Preface

Lines 60–164. Nevins defines the book as a Ford Motor Company history, an automotive-industry history, and a partial biography of Henry Ford. He emphasizes that motor cars and automobile factories transformed modern economic and social life, explains Columbia University’s independent role in the project, and identifies the Ford Archives and oral histories as major source foundations.

Chapter I — The Road: The Wheel

Lines 699–1531. The opening chapter places the automobile in the long American struggle to conquer distance. It moves from trails, packhorses, crude roads, wagons, canals, and railroads to the neglected condition of rural roads, making the modern highway and motor car appear as solutions to a national transport problem rather than as sudden novelties.

Chapter II — The Pioneers

Lines 1532–2216. Nevins introduces early automotive pioneers such as the Duryeas, Haynes, Olds, and Winton, then turns to Henry Ford’s birth near Dearborn during the Civil War. The chapter roots Ford in Irish immigrant, Michigan frontier, and Nowlin/Ford family settings, treating pioneer conditions as part of the mental world that shaped later mechanical ambition.

Chapter III — The Childhood of an Industrialist

Lines 2217–2958. This chapter connects Ford’s childhood to Detroit and the machine age rising in the Midwest. It covers William and Mary Ford, farm holdings, Ford’s siblings, school life, early mechanical experiments, and especially the influence of Mary Ford before her death in 1876.

Chapter IV — Youth and the Machines

Lines 2959–3786. Ford’s encounter with a portable steam engine becomes the chapter’s central awakening. Nevins broadens from Ford’s watch-repair skill and the 1876 Centennial Exposition to the history of steam and gas engines, Lenoir, Otto, Brayton, and Ford’s decision to leave farm life for Detroit in 1879.

Chapter V — A Persistent Apprentice

Lines 3787–4539. The young Ford enters industrial Detroit through the Michigan Car Company, the Flower brothers, Detroit Drydock, watch repair, stationary engines, Westinghouse engine work, and business college. The chapter makes Detroit’s shop culture and Ford’s persistent apprenticeship the bridge between farm youth and automotive experimentation.

Chapter VI — The Broadening Stream

Lines 4540–5660. Nevins widens the field to steam, gas, gasoline, the four-cycle engine, Siegfried Marcus, George B. Selden, Daimler, Benz, and other European and American developments. Ford’s courtship and marriage to Clara Bryant, sawmill work, engine repair, and decision to return to Detroit place him inside a growing international stream of road-carriage experiment.

Chapter VII — The Eve of Revolution

Lines 5661–6644. Ford works as night engineer at Edison Illuminating Company while Detroit becomes a more favorable industrial center. The chapter follows Daimler, Benz, Panhard & Levassor, Roger, Duryea, Olds, Haynes, Maxim, and early races in France and Chicago, showing that Ford’s experiments occurred amid accelerating transatlantic automotive activity.

Chapter VIII — A Horseless Carriage

Lines 6645–7800. Ford builds and runs his quadricycle in 1896 after experiments with small engines and help from Charles B. King, Barthel, Cato, Bishop, and Huff. The chapter compares Ford’s work with Duryea, Haynes, Olds, Winton, and Edison’s encouragement, ending with Ford selling the quadricycle and beginning a new car.

Chapter IX — Time of Experiment

Lines 7801–8771. Ford seeks backers, demonstrates a new car, and forms the Detroit Automobile Company while leaving Edison Illuminating. Nevins follows local support from William C. Maybury and William H. Murphy, the company’s failure in 1900, the bicycle’s influence on car manufacture, and Ford’s shift toward racing as a way to recover reputation.

Chapter X — Racing: A Prelude

Lines 8772–10089. Ford’s 1901 race against Winton, the Henry Ford Company, conflict with Murphy, Henry M. Leland’s role, Ford’s withdrawal, Tom Cooper, Barney Oldfield, and the new racer all make racing a prelude to the final company. The chapter shows reputation, publicity, family life, and investor confidence converging before Ford Motor Company.

Chapter XI — The Ford Motor Company

Lines 10090–11875. Nevins narrates Ford’s association with Alexander Malcomson, Harold Wills, James Couzens, the Dodge Brothers, John S. Gray, and the investors who formed Ford Motor Company with only $28,000 paid in. Model A, early bankruptcy risk, dealers, Couzens’s office role, Ford’s technical role, demand, defects, and first dividends define the company’s first success.

Chapter XII — A Battle for the Cheap Car

Lines 11876–13207. Ford battles internally for a light, inexpensive car against Malcomson’s preference for higher-priced models. The chapter covers Detroit’s automotive rise, good roads, Models A and B, Piquette Avenue, Galamb, Sorensen, Seeman, Emde, dealer growth, the Ford Manufacturing Company maneuver, and the stockholder struggle that secured the cheap-car strategy.

Chapter XIII — The Shadow of Monopoly

Lines 13208–15003. The Selden patent case dominates the chapter. Nevins explains the Electric Vehicle Company, Association of Licensed Automobile Manufacturers, Ford’s rebuffed licensing request, R. A. Parker’s advice to fight, Ford’s advertising counterattack, legal evidence, the Selden buggy, the Ford-Lenoir car, and the propaganda war around automotive monopoly.

Chapter XIV — Forerunner: Model N

Lines 15004–16321. The Model N gives Ford a sturdy, low-priced success before Model T. The chapter covers Malcomson’s surrender, Couzens’s acquisition of stock, merger of Ford Manufacturing and Ford Motor Company, Norval Hawkins’s sales role, Ford’s racing interests, vanadium steel, and planning for a still cheaper car.

Chapter XV — New Growth, New Problems

Lines 16322–17828. The panic of 1907, weak-firm failures, Highland Park planning, Canadian and British expansion, Percival Perry, H. B. White in Europe, P. E. Martin and Charles Sorensen in manufacturing, labor conditions in Detroit, antiunion employers, I.W.W. activity, and Ford plant working conditions frame the company just before Model T’s ascent.

Chapter XVI — The Advent of Model T

Lines 17829–19059. Model T receives its central chapter: advance publicity, Ford, Galamb, Wills, experimental-room work, vanadium steel, transmission, magneto, carburetor, bearings, tires, branches, dealers, service, the New York-Seattle race, branch assembly plants, British success, and Durant’s proposed merger into what became General Motors.

Chapter XVII — No Monopoly

Lines 19060–20481. The Selden patent battle reaches judgment and appeal. Judge Hough first supports the patent, the A.L.A.M. celebrates, Ford appeals, Judge Walter C. Noyes delivers a decision favorable to Ford, license payments stop, the industry is freed for broader activity, Ford buys the Keim plant, and the Ford and Couzens families adjust to wealth.

Chapter XVIII — A Lever to Move the World

Lines 20482–21848. Highland Park and mass production become the chapter’s subject. Nevins follows the move to the plant, building and machinery layout, Keim Mills, Knudsen, Smith, Lee, sequential production, conveyor belts, magneto/engine/chassis assemblies, Clarence Avery, 1912–1917 output gains, the Dodge contract break, and full adaptation to mass production.

Chapter XIX — The Anatomy of Success

Lines 21849–23240. Nevins analyzes the elements of Ford’s success: modernization of automobiles, self-starters, lights, left-hand drive, roads and license/gasoline revenues, the Lincoln Highway, Model T price cuts, parts service, mass-production economies, young executives, branch assembly, process improvement, sales organization, and the 1914 reduction to a $440 basic car.

Chapter XX — The Five-Dollar Day

Lines 23241–24529. The chapter places Ford’s 1914 wage announcement inside national labor conditions, declining unionism, the Employers’ Association of Detroit, immigrant labor supply, John R. Lee’s wage system, profit-sharing experiments, swelling profits, public praise and criticism, and debates over whether Ford, Couzens, Clara Ford, or Percival Perry shaped the measure.

Chapter XXI — The Company and the Worker

Lines 24530–25724. Nevins follows the immediate rush of job applicants, eligibility rules, women and clerical workers, labor costs, morale, beneficial services, wartime/living-cost complications, the Sociological Department, home investigations, criticism of privacy invasion, and Ford policies toward handicapped workers, ex-criminals, adjustment, and advancement.

Chapter XXII — Rising Dangers

Lines 25725–26572. The final chapter covers 1915–1916 production increases, price cuts, Ford at fifty-three, his dynamic philosophy, the end of the Dodge contract, operating committees, Edsel, the shift toward absolute personal power, Ford’s mechanical brilliance, intellectual limits, aloofness, inconsistent judgment outside his field, and danger from weaker advisers.

Notes, Appendices, Bibliography, and Index

Lines 26573–33690. The back matter is substantial. Notes run chapter by chapter; appendices preserve supporting evidence such as Ford family genealogy, production/sales/materials data, early stockholders, branch organization, and fiscal figures; the bibliography describes Ford Archives, oral histories, Detroit Public Library, University of Michigan, and related source collections; the index runs to the final OCR pages.

Coverage Guide

Automobiles, roads, and the American transport problem

Chapters I, VI, VII, and VIII connect the automobile to older road, wagon, canal, railroad, steam, gas-engine, and gasoline traditions. This source can support future pages on roads, good-roads reform, early automobiles, and the shift from railroad-dominated transport to motorized mobility.

Henry Ford and Ford Motor Company

Chapters II–XII provide the core biography and company-formation arc: Dearborn origins, farm childhood, Detroit apprenticeship, Edison Illuminating, the quadricycle, failed companies, racing, Malcomson, Couzens, Wills, the Dodges, Model A, and the victory of the cheap-car strategy. These chapters are the foundation for future Henry Ford and Ford Motor Company entity pages.

Patent monopoly and industry freedom

Chapters XIII and XVII are the main Selden patent sections. They connect legal strategy, Ford advertising, A.L.A.M. power, court evidence, Judge Hough, Judge Noyes, and the freeing of the industry from a licensing regime.

Mass production and Highland Park

Chapters XVI–XIX are the key mass-production cluster. They cover Model T, Highland Park, Keim Mills, conveyor belts, component assembly, process synchronization, branch assembly, price reductions, and the managerial/technical team behind Ford’s output gains.

Labor policy and Progressive Era industrial welfare

Chapters XX and XXI are the strongest labor sections: the five-dollar day, immigrant labor, Detroit employer associations, profit-sharing, welfare work, Sociological Department home investigations, and Ford’s attempt to link wages, morality, stability, and production.

Character, autocracy, and future risk

Chapter XXII is the interpretive pivot at the end of the volume. It presents Ford as mechanically brilliant and commercially successful, but increasingly autocratic, aloof, and vulnerable to poor advisers and unsound judgments outside his practical field.

Notes on Scope

This is a company-sponsored but Columbia-administered modern secondary history with heavy archival use. It should be mined as a major source for early Ford and automotive industry history through the 1910s, while remembering that it is Volume I of a larger Ford project and not the full late-life story of Henry Ford.
