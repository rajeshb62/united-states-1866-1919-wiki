Overview

Lindbergh writes as a Progressive-era critic of concentrated finance during the public controversy over the Money Trust and banking reform. He argues that commodity prices, bank credit, interest, dividends, rent, and short selling are shaped less by natural production and demand than by a manipulated money system controlled by Wall Street financiers. The book is valuable for this wiki as a near-contemporary polemic against the financial order just before the Federal Reserve Act, and as a bridge between Populist-style monetary criticism, Progressive regulation, congressional investigation, and wartime-era anti-finance politics.

The source pairs directly with Why Is Your Country at War? because Lindbergh later presents the 1917 war crisis as a continuation of the conditions he attacked in 1913. It should also be mined alongside The House of Morgan for the institutional finance side of the same debates and Recent History of the United States for the broader context of banking reform, the 1907 panic, Aldrich-Vreeland, and Progressive politics.

Narrative Source Flow

Front Matter and Contents

Lines 1–116. The title and copyright pages identify Lindbergh as author and National Capital Press as publisher. The contents page lays out the source as a sequence of practical political-economy arguments: general observations, introduction, diagnosis, banking/currency/Money Trust, interest and rents, short selling, political aspects, deposit guarantees, money, remedies, and appendices.

General Observations

Lines 118–206. Lindbergh begins from the claim that material production and consumption are relatively stable, while market prices and commercial relations fluctuate sharply because of human institutions. He frames banking and currency reform, industrial relations, and political reform as connected problems created by a money system that makes the people subordinate to concentrated financial power.

Introduction and In Contemplation

Lines 207–709. The opening argument presents the money problem as the first of several linked studies in business, politics, and social relations. Lindbergh attacks inherited financial habits, asks readers to examine who benefits from existing credit arrangements, and argues that public credit should serve the people rather than private financial groups.

What Is the Matter with Us?

Lines 710–914. This section poses the book’s diagnosis in direct political language: the problem is not merely technical banking procedure but a social order in which common productive capacity is turned into claims for private tribute. It is useful for future pages on Progressive monetary critique, popular anti-monopoly thought, and the political language of financial reform.

Currency, Banking, and the Money Trust

Lines 915–4276. This is the central section. Lindbergh discusses bank organization, national-bank capital, reserves, deposits, credit creation, stock ownership, clearing-house power, Wall Street influence, and congressional inquiry into the Money Trust. He treats the banking system as a political institution that permits a small group to expand or contract credit and thereby shape markets, production, and livelihoods.

Interest, Dividends and Rents; Short Selling

Lines 4277–5191. Lindbergh extends the critique from banks to claims on production. Interest, dividends, rents, watered stock, and speculative short selling are presented as mechanisms that transfer the fruits of labor and production to non-producing claimants. The short-selling section is especially useful for the source’s view of speculative markets as a form of social control rather than ordinary commerce.

Political Aspect, Bank Guarantees, and Money

Lines 5192–6010. These sections move from economic diagnosis to political remedy. Lindbergh discusses the relation between financial power and party politics, government guarantee of bank deposits, the meaning of money, and the way gold and credit acquire value through public authority.

The Remedy, Two-Fold Remedy, and Practical System

Lines 6011–8818. Lindbergh proposes a replacement system in which the public, through government, controls credit rather than leaving monetary power to private banks. This long remedy section should be mined for concrete proposals, including his distinction between a moral-political remedy and the practical machinery of a better financial system.

Appendices A–B

Lines 8819–10311. Appendix A responds to reader questions and includes correspondence and discussion bearing on banking reform. Appendix B reproduces excerpts from Lindbergh’s arguments before the House Rules Committee and House debate connected to the campaign for a special Money Trust investigation.

Coverage Guide

Use this source for Progressive-era banking and currency politics, Money Trust rhetoric, critiques of Wall Street, public-credit proposals, and the intellectual background to congressional banking reform. It is less a neutral manual of banking than a political-economic pamphlet from inside the reform fight; claims about institutions should be cross-checked against banking histories and congressional material before becoming entity-page facts.

  • Lines 60–72: Lindbergh’s prefatory thesis that daily price fluctuations prove manipulation by the Money Trust.
  • Lines 147–187: banking/currency, industrial relations, and political reform stated as connected public problems.

Notes on Scope

This is a polemical reform source written by an active political critic. It is strongest as evidence for Lindbergh’s own anti-Money Trust argument and for Progressive-era monetary language; it should not be treated as a neutral institutional account without corroborating sources.
