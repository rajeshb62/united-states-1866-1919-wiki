Panic of 1907

The Panic of 1907 was an acute six-week financial crisis in October and November 1907 that brought the American commercial banking system to near-total collapse. Precipitated by the failure of a speculative attempt to corner the stock of the United Copper Company, the panic triggered runs on major New York trust companies, paralyzing the call-money market, crashing stock prices, and causing hundreds of bank failures across the nation. In the absence of a federal central bank, seventy-year-old private banker j-p-morgan-sr assumed personal command of the financial community, organizing private rescue pools from his personal library, allocating liquidity, and brokering the absorption of the Tennessee Coal, Iron & Railroad Company by the United States Steel Corporation with the assent of President theodore-roosevelt. The crisis demonstrated the extreme instability of an inelastic national banking structure, leading directly to the passage of the Aldrich-Vreeland Act of 1908 and the creation of the modern Federal Reserve System under banking-reform-and-the-federal-reserve-act.

The Copper Speculation and the Run on Trusts

The immediate spark of the crisis was a reckless stock manipulation scheme engineered by copper magnate F. Augustus Heinze and Wall Street speculator Charles W. Morse. Heinze attempted to corner the shares of the United Copper Company on the New York Curb market. When the speculative pool collapsed on October 16, 1907, the stock plummeted from 60to10 a share, ruining Heinze’s brokerage firm and precipitating runs on commercial banks associated with Heinze and Morse, including the Mercantile National Bank.

The New York Clearing House agreed to support the affected national banks provided Heinze and Morse resigned all institutional offices. However, the contagion swiftly leaped from commercial banks to trust companies. Unlike national banks, which were subject to strict federal regulation and required to maintain twenty-five percent cash reserves, state-chartered trust companies operated with minimal cash reserves, engaged in risky stock-market speculation, and were excluded from membership in the Clearing House.

Morgan’s Library Command

The Knickerbocker’s collapse ignited a systemic run on other New York trust companies, most severely the Trust Company of America and the Lincoln Trust Company. At this critical juncture, J. Pierpont Morgan stepped forward to take autocratic command of the crisis.

Operating from his opulent Italian Renaissance library at 219 Madison Avenue, Morgan assembled a team of young financial lieutenants—including Henry P. Davison, Benjamin Strong Jr. (later the first governor of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York), and Thomas W. Lamont. He dispatched audit teams to inspect the securities of besieged institutions, declaring: “If the Trust Company of America is solvent, we will save it; if it is not, it must fail.”

Over the next two weeks, Morgan executed a sequence of dramatic rescue operations:

  1. The Trust Company Pool: On the evening of October 23, after audits established that the Trust Company of America possessed sound collateral, Morgan raised a $23,000,000 emergency pool from national bank presidents and the federal Treasury (dispatched by Secretary of the Treasury George B. Cortelyou). Cash was physically carted in drays through the streets to pay waiting depositors, averting immediate collapse.
  2. Rescuing the Stock Exchange: On the afternoon of October 24, the President of the New York Stock Exchange, Ransom H. Thomas, rushed into Morgan’s office to announce that call money was unavailable at any interest rate and that the Exchange would be forced to close within twenty minutes. Morgan summoned the city’s leading bank presidents, sat them down, and gave them ten minutes to pledge $25,000,000 in liquidity. Within sixteen minutes, the funds were on the Exchange floor, loans were offered at ten percent, and panic selling halted.
  3. The City of New York Rescue: On October 29, Mayor George B. The Tennessee Coal & Iron Transaction

The climax of the panic arrived on the weekend of November 2–3. The venerable Wall Street brokerage firm of Moore & Schley faced bankruptcy with over $25,000,000 in call loans. The firm held as principal collateral a controlling majority block of stock in the Tennessee Coal, Iron & Railroad Company (TC&I), the largest steel and iron producer in the South and a prime competitor of the United States Steel Corporation. If Moore & Schley failed, banks would dump TC&I shares on the market, triggering insolvencies throughout the financial community.

Morgan formulated a masterstroke: U.S. Steel would purchase the TC&I stock from Moore & Schley, exchanging 30,000,000 in high-grade U.S. Steel gold bonds for the shares, thereby extinguishing Moore & Schley's debt and stabilizing the market. To ensure the financial community's total cooperation, Morgan locked over one hundred bank and trust company executives inside his library on Saturday night, November 2. At 4:45 on Sunday morning, he presented a final contract for a collective 25,000,000 trust rescue fund, declaring to Edward King, president of the Union Trust Company: “There is the place to sign, and here is the pen.” King signed, followed by the others, and the doors were unlocked.

However, the acquisition of TC&I threatened to violate the Sherman Antitrust Act by expanding U.S. Steel’s market share past sixty percent. On Sunday evening, U.S. Steel executives Elbert H. Gary and Henry Clay Frick boarded a special midnight train to Washington. On Monday morning, November 4, they met President Theodore Roosevelt at the White House before the stock exchange opened. Gary and Frick assured the President that the purchase was an act of civic duty to avert a national financial catastrophe, not an effort to monopolize the steel market.

Roosevelt agreed not to interpose any objection to the merger, dictated a memorandum confirming that the government would not prosecute the acquisition under antitrust laws, and telegraphed his decision to New York. The stock market rallied immediately, and the panic subsided.

Aftermath and the Road to the Federal Reserve

The Panic of 1907 produced a profound shift in American political economy. While the business establishment celebrated Morgan as a patriotic hero, the episode exposed the intolerable vulnerability of a modern capitalist empire dependent upon the personal will and private fortune of a single aging banker.

Critics—including insurgent congressman charles-a-lindbergh-sr—charged that the panic had been orchestrated, or at least ruthlessly exploited, by the house-of-morgan to eliminate independent trust companies and absorb valuable industrial properties like TC&I at distress prices. Public suspicion culminated in the 1912 Pujo Committee investigation into the money-trust.

Statutorily, the panic forced Congress to acknowledge the rigidity of the national currency. In May 1908, Congress passed the Aldrich-Vreeland Act, authorizing commercial banks to issue emergency currency backed by municipal bonds and commercial paper during crises, and establishing the National Monetary Commission under Senator Nelson W. Aldrich. The Commission’s exhaustive multi-volume investigations into European central banking laid the direct institutional groundwork for the Glass-Owen Federal Reserve Act of 1913, terminating the era of private banker hegemony and establishing the sovereign authority of banking-reform-and-the-federal-reserve-act.
