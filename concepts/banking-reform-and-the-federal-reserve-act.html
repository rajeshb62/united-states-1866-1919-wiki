Banking Reform and the Federal Reserve Act

The Banking Reform and the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 established the modern central banking system of the United States, concluding a half-century struggle over the control of the national currency and credit. Enacted on December 23, 1913, under President woodrow-wilson, the Federal Reserve Act created a decentralized central bank comprising twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks supervised by a public Federal Reserve Board in Washington. The legislation resolved the chronic currency inelasticity and reserve-pyramiding that had triggered recurring panics—most acutely the panic-of-1893 and the panic-of-1907—while forging an ideological compromise between Wall Street bankers who demanded centralized private financial control and agrarian radicals who demanded sovereign democratic control over the money supply to destroy the money-trust.

The Defects of the National Banking System

Since the Civil War, American commercial banking had operated under the National Banking Acts of 1863–1864. While the system created a uniform national currency backed by federal government bonds, it suffered from two fatal structural defects:

  1. Inelastic Currency: The volume of national bank notes in circulation was determined not by the fluctuating commercial needs of the nation’s trade, but by the volume and market price of federal government bonds. In autumn, when midwestern and southern farmers harvested and shipped wheat, cotton, and corn, demand for cash soared, creating acute seasonal credit crunches. During economic panics, when depositors demanded cash, the money supply could not expand, forcing banks to call in loans, raise interest rates, and suspend cash payments.
  2. Pyramiding of Reserves: Country banks were permitted to deposit up to three-fifths of their legal reserves in “reserve city” banks, which in turn deposited their reserves in “central reserve city” banks in New York, Chicago, and St. Louis. To earn interest on these idle funds, New York commercial banks poured billions of dollars into call loans on the Wall Street Stock Exchange to finance stock speculation. When country banks withdrew their deposits in autumn harvest seasons or during liquidity crises, New York banks were forced to call loans, causing stock market crashes and pulling down solvent banks across the country.

The Jekyll Island Conference and the Aldrich Plan

Following the near-collapse of Wall Street in the panic-of-1907, Congress passed the Aldrich-Vreeland Act of 1908, authorizing emergency currency and creating the National Monetary Commission under Rhode Island Senator Nelson W. Aldrich.

In November 1910, Senator Aldrich, accompanied by a select group of Wall Street bankers—including Henry P. Davison (senior partner of J. P. Morgan & Company), Frank A. Vanderlip (president of the National City Bank of New York), Paul M. Warburg (partner in Kuhn, Loeb & Company), and A. Piatt Andrew (Assistant Secretary of the Treasury)—traveled in total secrecy aboard a private railway car to the remote Jekyll Island Club off the coast of Georgia.

Over ten days of intensive drafting, the group formulated what became known as the Aldrich Plan. The plan proposed a single, centralized institution: the National Reserve Association, headquartered in Washington, with fifteen regional branches. The institution would hold commercial bank reserves, issue currency based on commercial paper, and act as a lender of last resort. Crucially, the National Reserve Association was to be owned, governed, and controlled exclusively by private bankers, with the President of the United States possessing only nominal veto power over the governor.

When introduced in Congress in 1912, the Aldrich Plan was fiercely denounced by Progressive reformers, Democrats, and agrarian insurgents like charles-a-lindbergh-sr, who condemned it as a blatant attempt to establish a private central bank controlled by the money-trust.

Wilson, Glass, and the Bryan Compromise

The election of Woodrow Wilson and Democratic majorities in Congress in the election-of-1912, accompanied by the sensational revelations of the Pujo Committee’s investigation into the Money Trust, made banking reform the paramount domestic issue of 1913.

Representative Carter Glass of Virginia, chairman of the House Committee on Banking and Currency, and economics expert H. Parker Willis drafted a Democratic alternative. Glass rejected a single centralized bank, proposing instead a decentralized system of twenty or more regional reserve banks owned and governed by private member banks, with minimal federal coordination.

When Glass presented his draft to President Wilson in December 1912, Wilson endorsed the regional architecture but insisted on an indispensable public crowning feature: an overarching Federal Reserve Board in Washington appointed entirely by the President of the United States.

This public board ignited a fierce rebellion within the Democratic Party:

  • Wall Street Bankers: Demanded representation on the governing board, arguing that since private commercial banks provided the capital, private bankers should control the system. Glass concurred, warning that a political board would invite partisan corruption.
  • Agrarian Radicals: Led by Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan and supported by insurgent westerners like Lindbergh, radicals demanded that the governing board be one hundred percent public, and that the new emergency currency notes must be direct legal-tender obligations of the United States Government, not private bank IOUs.

Wilson achieved a masterful political compromise. Meeting with Glass and Bryan at the White House, Wilson sided firmly with Bryan on principle, asking Glass: “Which of you gentlemen would have me place railroad presidents on the Interstate Commerce Commission?” Wilson agreed that the Federal Reserve Board would be entirely appointed by the President with Senate confirmation, and that Federal Reserve Notes would be issued by the federal government and redeemable in gold on demand at the Treasury. In return, Bryan gave the bill his enthusiastic public endorsement, holding the southern and western agrarian factions in line.

Legislative Enactment and the Federal Reserve System

Introduced in the House by Glass and in the Senate by Robert L. Owen of Oklahoma, the Federal Reserve Act faced intense lobbying from the American Bankers Association, which condemned the measure as “socialistic” and “tyrannical.” However, Wilson held Congress in continuous special session throughout the sweltering Washington summer of 1913, exercising iron party discipline. The bill passed the House by a vote of 287 to 85, passed the Senate 54 to 34, and was signed into law by President Wilson on December 23, 1913.

The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 established:

  1. Twelve Regional Banks: Rather than a single central bank, the nation was divided into twelve Federal Reserve districts, each with a Federal Reserve Bank (in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, and San Francisco), owned by member commercial banks who subscribed six percent of their capital.
  2. Elastic Currency: The act created Federal Reserve Notes, issued to regional banks against commercial paper and a mandatory forty percent gold reserve. When business expanded, banks discounted commercial paper at the Federal Reserve to obtain new currency; when business contracted, the paper was repaid and notes retired, providing an elastic circulating medium that expanded and contracted with commercial demand.
  3. Mobilized Reserves: All national banks were required to join the system, and state banks were permitted to join, pooling their legal reserves in the regional Federal Reserve Banks, terminating the dangerous pyramiding of reserves in Wall Street call-loan markets.
  4. The Federal Reserve Board: A seven-member public board in Washington, consisting of the Secretary of the Treasury, the Comptroller of the Currency, and five members appointed by the President for ten-year terms, endowed with broad supervisory power to review rediscount rates, supervise reserve transfers, and examine operations.

Historical Significance

The Federal Reserve System began operations in November 1914, just as the outbreak of World War I destabilized international finance. Under the leadership of Benjamin Strong Jr., governor of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the system mobilized American credit, stabilized the New York financial markets, and successfully financed the enormous war loan drives of world-war-i-mobilization in 1917–1918.

While critics like Lindbergh continued to warn that private financial syndicates exercised undue influence within the regional reserve banks, the Federal Reserve Act stands as the crowning legislative achievement of Woodrow Wilson’s domestic program. It ended fifty years of recurring monetary panics, established public sovereign authority over the national credit, and created the institutional financial apparatus that enabled the United States to emerge from the First World War as the financial colossus of the twentieth century.
